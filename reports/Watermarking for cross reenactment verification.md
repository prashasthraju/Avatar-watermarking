# Bind Declared Drivers, Then Let Biometrics Check Them

As of October 2026, nobody has published a system that does what this plan proposes: binding a driver claim and a target-identity claim to a reenactment video, then checking the driver claim against an independent motion biometric. The plan's central combination is therefore open territory, but it rests on two half-solved halves that it currently over-trusts. Proactive face watermarks are almost all image-only and tested on CelebA-HQ crops. Only six report any reenactment test at all, the newest reenactment generator used is FSRT (CVPR 2024), and none has been tested against LivePortrait, GAGAvatar or HunyuanPortrait. Driver biometrics ("avatar fingerprinting") reach only about 0.85–0.97 AUC in-domain and lose up to roughly 20 AUC points under generator shift. None reports EER, and none has been attacked by mimicry or motion replay. The plan also has fixable conceptual errors. Its diagram feeds an *estimated* driver identity into the watermark, which destroys the independence of the two channels. Its "same vs different identity" output is the wrong question for authorized cross-reenactment. It cites generation-time methods that cannot run on non-diffusion reenactors. And it omits a threat model, even though open-source generators make a missing watermark meaningless and white-box forgery is cheaper than removal. For a B.Tech project, the feasible and still-novel path is LivePortrait plus AVAPrintDB. Start with a post-hoc VideoSeal baseline using per-segment keyed payloads, then attempt an SPDMark-style LoRA watermark in LivePortrait's SPADE decoder. The watermark should carry a short random ID that resolves to a signed C2PA manifest holding the claims. The biometric should be a landmark-graph fingerprinting verifier, and the analysis should be forensic and offline rather than in live videoconferencing.

## Two mature halves, one missing bridge

### Proactive face watermarks rarely see reenactment, and never modern reenactors

The proactive deepfake-watermark literature comprises roughly 20 methods, and "identity" means three incompatible things across it. **Face identity** is an ArcFace-style embedding (Zhao et al. WACV 2023, CMark, One-Identity-One-Key, SVS-WM). **Source or provenance ID** is an arbitrary file, owner or generator code (SepMark, SIDT, SAiW, GIFGuard). **Face geometry** means landmarks (LampMark, LIDMark). No method encodes a driver identity. LIDMark, the plan's headline "identity" citation, embeds 136 landmark coordinates plus a **16-bit truncated SHA-256 of the image filename**, which its authors use "for convenience … to simulate this tracing scenario". It is image-only, tested on CelebA-HQ and LFW, has no reenactment experiment, and lists video as future work ([LIDMark](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.23523)).

Only six methods test reenactment at all, all on single images at 128–512 px. The generators are Face2Face (2016), GANimation (2018), StyleMask, HyperReenact (2023) and FSRT (2024). LampMark, a robust mark trained without reenactment and tested on it, gives the clearest documented gap. In its own Table 3, average recovery is **97.44% on face swaps versus 73.61% on "reenactment" at 128 px**, and 96.46% versus 74.89% at 256 px. Its detection AUC still stays at or above 98.6% on HyperReenact and StyleMask, because the recovered mark is compared against landmarks re-derived from the content ([LampMark](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17209)). Semi-fragile designs, whose job is to break, report near-perfect reenactment detection: FractalForensics reaches AUC 99.99% on StyleMask and HyperReenact with no deepfakes in training ([FractalForensics](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09451)), and DeepForgeSeal's bit recovery falls to 0.06 under HyperReenact ([DeepForgeSeal](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04949)). DiffMark is the only robust payload shown to survive a transformer reenactor, with BER of 0.12% (128 px) and 4.05% (256 px) on FSRT ([DiffMark](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.01428)).

A full-text search of about 20 papers found **no proactive watermark evaluated against LivePortrait, EMOPortraits, X-Portrait, HunyuanPortrait, FOMM, TPSMM or face-vid2vid**. The only proactive defense tested on current off-the-shelf video tools is TaintedPixels, against MuseTalk, Roop and FaceFusion. It uses a perturbation that becomes visible after manipulation and carries no recoverable payload ([TaintedPixels](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.27492)). GIFGuard is the only temporal face watermark with payload recovery, and it covers 10-frame GIFs under face swaps only ([GIFGuard](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.26519)). A caution on evidence quality: one group (vpsg-research) produced DiffMark, LIDMark, GIFGuard and One-Identity-One-Key, and LIDMark's baseline rows are identical to DiffMark's. The baselines were therefore copied rather than re-run, so the 2025–2026 numbers are not independent replications ([LIDMark](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.23523); [DiffMark](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.01428)).

### Video watermarks fall into four families, each tied to a generator architecture

Generation-time video watermarking is mature, but each family assumes a particular generator. **Post-hoc pixel** methods (VideoSeal, LoT-Pass) work on any video. **Decoder fine-tuning** (Stable Signature, VidSig, SPDMark) needs a learned decoder you control. **Initial-noise** methods (Tree-Ring, Gaussian Shading, VideoShield, VideoMark, SIGMark) need a diffusion or flow sampler, and verification has to invert through the generator. **Motion-latent** marks (MoSign) need a motion VAE.

VideoSeal reaches 0.99 bit accuracy clean on real SA-V video, 0.85 under H.264/H.265, and 0.73 under combined codec, crop and brightness attacks. It embeds the same message in every frame and averages across frames, so it survives temporal edits but cannot detect them ([VideoSeal](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.09492v1)). Its repository now ships a 256-bit v1.0 model as the default and labels the 96-bit model "legacy" ([videoseal repo](https://github.com/facebookresearch/videoseal)). SPDMark is the most transferable template for this project. It adds LoRA bases to 14 decoder blocks and lets a key select the combination per frame, giving 28 bits per frame from HMAC-SHA256-derived messages. Hungarian matching recovers frame order with drop and insert localization F1 of about 0.995–0.999. It averages 0.935 robust bit accuracy on SVD-XT, with recompression at 0.880 and screen recording at 0.837, and trained in about 8 GPU-hours on one A6000 ([SPDMark](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.12090v2)). The noise-inversion methods post the best clean and temporal numbers, but verification costs a full diffusion inversion: SIGMark needs about 30 s of CPU decoding plus inversion per sample ([SIGMark](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.02882v1)). Cross-paper comparisons conflict badly. SIGMark measures VideoMark at 0.51–0.52 under temporal disturbances on HunyuanVideo, while VideoMark reports about 1.000 on ModelScope ([SIGMark](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.02882v1); [VideoMark](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.16359v3)). Treat any single paper's table as specific to its generator.

### Driver biometrics exist in two lineages, both passive

"Who drove this avatar?" has been studied only passively, in about seven papers.

**Lineage A** works on rendered video. Prashnani et al. (NVIDIA, ECCV 2024) defined *avatar fingerprinting*. Their method feeds 126-landmark pairwise distances over 71-frame clips into a temporal CNN with a contrastive loss, and compares against the driver's self-reenacted clips. It scores an **average AUC of 0.85, and 0.83 on unseen generators**. Its training data, NVFAIR, covers 161 identities and more than 650,000 videos from face-vid2vid, LIA and TPS, and the model was never released ([Prashnani et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.03713v3); [AVAPrintDB](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.26934v2)). BiDA Lab followed with a landmark graph network (IJCB 2025) and then **AVAPrintDB** (Pattern Recognition 2026). AVAPrintDB has 109 identities and 66,069 self- and cross-reenactments, every target–driver pair rendered by GAGAvatar, LivePortrait and HunyuanPortrait. Trial lists and checkpoints are public. In-domain fusion AUC reaches 95.2% (CREMA-D, LivePortrait), but cross-generator transfer costs up to 20.4 points and cross-dataset transfer up to 28.9 points. Landmark features are the most generator-robust ([AVAPrintDB](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.26934v2)). Chapariniya et al. (FG 2026 TrustFA workshop) reach 0.877 on NVFAIR from raw 128×128 frames. Their ablation shows that motion, not static appearance, carries the signal: 0.877 with inter-frame differencing against 0.610 with static features ([Chapariniya et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.23247v1)).

**Lineage B** works on the *transmitted pose-expression latent* in AI videoconferencing. Unmasking Puppeteers (NeurIPS 2025) compares the live latent against the reference portrait sent at call setup, with no enrollment. It reaches **0.968 average AUC in-domain (Table 1 pool)**, and in its cross-domain experiment AUC moves from .945 to .925 when training on NVC and testing on CREMA-D/RAVDESS. It needs about 40 frames, and runs at about 75 FPS. Its repository is a README-only placeholder under a non-commercial license ([Unmasking Puppeteers](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.03548v4); [MISL repo](https://github.com/MISLresearch/Unmasking-Puppeteers-Neurips25)).

No paper in either lineage reports EER or TAR@FAR. None tests an adaptive adversary. None combines its biometric with watermarking. Prashnani et al. explicitly position their method as "a passive technique that does not rely on active watermarks" ([Prashnani et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.03713v3)).

### Provenance standards treat watermarks as pointers, not proofs

The current C2PA specification is **2.4 (April 2026)**. Version 2.3 (December 2025) added live-video streaming, and version 2.2 added the Soft Binding Resolution API ([C2PA 2.4](https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.4/specs/C2PA_Specification.html); [C2PA 2.3](https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.3/specs/C2PA_Specification.html)). In C2PA a watermark is a *soft binding*: a lookup key a resolver uses to fetch a signed manifest. The spec states that "a soft binding shall not be used as a hard binding". The `c2pa.soft-binding` assertion supports per-block `timespan` and `region` scopes, which suit per-segment video marks ([C2PA 2.2](https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.2/specs/C2PA_Specification.html)). Soft bindings must use an algorithm on C2PA's approved list. Of its 28 entries, none is Meta's VideoSeal or PixelSeal ([soft-binding list](https://spec.c2pa.org/softbinding-alg-list/softbinding-algorithm-list.json)). The plan correctly says a valid watermark does not prove its claim. The literature adds a sharper warning: a valid manifest and a contradictory watermark can each pass verification independently, an "Integrity Clash" that only a joint cross-layer audit catches ([Nemecek et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02378)).

## Seven corrections the plan needs before any code is written

The plan's caution is sound. It says a watermark is not proof, that cross-reenactment is not inherently malicious, that unseen generators must be reported, and that the combination needs ablation. The design itself has seven problems.

**First, the architecture breaks the independence it claims.** The diagram runs the driving video through a motion encoder and feeds that "estimated driver identity" into the watermark module. The verifier's biometric then re-estimates the driver from the same motion. Both channels now derive from one signal and share its failure modes: a weakly expressive driver, a generator that drops identifying expressions, or a replayed victim motion. The driver claim must instead come from an **authenticated declaration**, such as a logged-in account, a device signature at capture, or a signed consent record. The biometric then *tests* that declaration. Only then is a disagreement between channels informative.

**Second, the verification comparison is mis-specified, and the error decides the project's scope.** The plan asks whether "the independently estimated driving identity match[es] the rendered target identity". That is the portrait-reference test of Unmasking Puppeteers, and it works only on the transmitted pose-expression latent, where identity leaks from the driver ([Unmasking Puppeteers](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.03548v4)). On rendered video, every working method compares against **enrolled reference motion of the claimed driver**, not against the target's face ([Prashnani et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.03713v3); [AVAPrintDB](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.26934v2)). Choosing published video therefore means choosing enrollment.

**Third, "same vs different identity" is the wrong primary output.** For authorized cross-reenactment, such as a dubbing actor driving a consenting presenter's avatar, a correct "different" verdict says nothing about legitimacy. The question users and regulators need answered is: *does the biometric agree with the declared driver, and is that driver within the target's signed consent scope?* Self- versus cross-reenactment then falls out as a special case, where the declared driver equals the target.

**Fourth, "identity- and landmark-conditioned watermarking" points at methods structurally unsuited to reenactment.** Landmark-bound marks (LampMark, LIDMark) flag a manipulation whenever geometry changes. Reenactment changes pose and expression by design, so these marks should flag *every* reenactment, authorized or not. Face-identity-bound marks have the opposite problem, because reenactment preserves the target's identity; Zhao et al. concede their method "performs poorly on StarGAN2 … because StarGAN2 does not modify face identity-related features" ([Zhao et al. WACV 2023](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/WACV2023/papers/Zhao_Proactive_Deepfake_Defence_via_Identity_Watermarking_WACV_2023_paper.pdf)). A mark on the source portrait largely survives LivePortrait, whose default config pastes the animated crop back onto the original image ([LivePortrait config](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KwaiVGI/LivePortrait/main/src/config/argument_config.py)), but it proves only "derived from this photo". Genuine and puppeteered outputs carry it alike. The landmark idea is still useful if inverted: the generator could sign the landmark trajectory it actually rendered, so that later re-driving or editing breaks the binding.

**Fifth, most of the cited generation-time methods cannot run on the likely generator.** SIGMark, VideoShield, VideoMark, Tree-Ring and Gaussian Shading all embed in diffusion initial noise and verify by inversion. LivePortrait is a warping model with a SPADE decoder ([LivePortrait](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.03168)), and GAGAvatar uses Gaussian splatting plus a neural renderer ([GAGAvatar](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.07971v1)). Neither has a noise latent to mark. The noise family does apply in principle to diffusion portrait animators: HunyuanPortrait runs on Stable Video Diffusion ([HunyuanPortrait](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.18860)). But the verifier would then have to invert without knowing the reference image or the motion conditioning, an open feasibility question. Decoder-tuning methods (SPDMark, Stable Signature, VidSig) are the family that transfers.

**Sixth, the plan has no threat model, and the open-source case undermines most of it.** If LivePortrait is public, anyone can render without the watermark, so a missing watermark proves nothing. VideoSeal's own paper concedes that post-hoc marking is "less secure, e.g., in the case of open-sourcing the generative model" ([VideoSeal](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.09492)). Weight-embedded marks are reported to be removable by fine-tuning ([Hu et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.07145); [RoboSignature](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.19834v1)). With a public extractor, white-box PGD drives **FNR and FPR to 1 for all four methods** in VideoMarkBench, and forgery needs smaller perturbations than removal ([VideoMarkBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.21620)). A payload that encodes only (session, driver) is content-agnostic, so it can be transplanted onto other content to frame an innocent driver. The defense is content binding. MetaSeal signs content features ([MetaSeal](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.10766)), and CSGuard ties embedding and verification to a secret matrix, cutting forgery success from 100% to 28.12% ([CSGuard](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.01479)). The honest claim for this system is **positive attestation from a controlled deployment**: "this clip came from our service, in this session, under this declared driver". It cannot detect unwatermarked fakes.

**Seventh, the payload puts claims and biometrics where they do not belong.** Raw capacity is 96–256 bits (VideoSeal v1.0 has 256), and error-correction overhead shrinks it further. A 64-byte EdDSA signature (512 bits) does not fit, so the bits can hold only an ID plus a truncated MAC ([videoseal repo](https://github.com/facebookresearch/videoseal)). Identity commitments derived from face embeddings are unsafe. Deep face templates have too little entropy: a reconstruction attack on fuzzy commitments recovers faces, and over 78% of reconstructed templates unlock the account at 0.1% FAR in the simplest scenario ([Keller et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13293v1)). IronMask leaks the template across multiple enrolments ([Zhu & Wang, PoPETs 2025](https://petsymposium.org/popets/2025/popets-2025-0154.php)). Deterministic IDs derived from a (target, driver) pair make every video of that pair linkable. The temporal plan has a related gap. VideoSeal and VidSig repeat one message per frame, so they *survive* deletion and reordering but cannot *detect* them; only per-frame or per-segment keyed messages (SPDMark, VideoMark, SIGMark) or positional templates (VideoShield) localize temporal tampering ([SPDMark](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.12090v2); [VideoShield](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.14195v2)). No paper evaluates frame-rate conversion or interpolation against any video watermark ([VideoMarkBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.21620)).

## Six genuine gaps, ranked by novelty per unit of effort

The search found no published system that binds a driver claim and a target claim in reenactment video. It found no watermark paper that reports self- and cross-reenactment separately, and no published watermark inside a non-diffusion reenactment generator; the passive fingerprinting papers themselves contain no watermarking component ([Prashnani et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.03713v3); [AVAPrintDB](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.26934v2)). The closest analogs each carry only one claim. SIDT hides the source face in a provider's face-swap output ([SIDT](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICME52920.2022.9859600)). SAiW encodes the generator class ([SAiW](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23178)). SVS-WM cross-verifies correlated identity and attribute marks of the *same* face ([SVS-WM](https://doi.org/10.1145/3746027.3755587)). MoSign embeds a keyed, nonce-bound MAC in a motion-VAE latent, but for body and VR motion, not faces ([MoSign](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29603)). Two USPTO patents on "talking head digital identity authentication" combine blockchain hashes with a watermark or logo but use no driver biometrics ([USPTO 12301728](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12301728)).

The novelty claim depends on how far the search reached. Full texts of One-Identity-One-Key, SVS-WM, Synth-Tracker and the ACM Computing Surveys review of proactive defenses ([CSUR](https://doi.org/10.1145/3771296)) were not retrieved, and patents were not searched systematically. Before claiming "first", the student should read those four in full, run Google Scholar "cited by" sweeps on Prashnani et al. and LampMark, and search patents for "avatar" with "watermark" and "driver".

Ranked by how much novelty each delivers per unit of effort, the gaps are:

**(1) Benchmark existing released watermarks against modern portrait animators.** LampMark, LIDMark, DiffMark, FractalForensics, SepMark and VideoSeal all have public code, and none has faced LivePortrait, GAGAvatar or HunyuanPortrait ([LampMark code](https://github.com/wangty1/LampMark); [LIDMark code](https://github.com/vpsg-research/LIDMark); [DiffMark code](https://github.com/vpsg-research/DiffMark)). Measure source-portrait survival with paste-back on and off, separately for self- and cross-reenactment. This is a few weeks of work and immediately publishable as an empirical finding. One useful nuance: at LampMark's ~74% reenactment bit accuracy ([LampMark](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17209)), exact payload recovery fails, but zero-bit presence detection should still work. Under an independent-bit-error assumption (a derived calculation, not a figure from the paper), ≥95 of 128 correct bits has a binomial tail of p ≈ 1.9×10⁻⁸.

**(2) Test whether watermarking erodes the biometric.** Fingerprinting depends on subtle inter-frame motion ([Chapariniya et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.23247v1)). Any watermark that adds temporal jitter or smooths micro-expressions could cut AVAPrintDB AUC. This is a cheap, clean experiment: run the released AVAPrintDB checkpoints on watermarked and unwatermarked renders, and it directly answers the plan's own "do the channels interfere?" question.

**(3) Embed a keyed per-frame watermark inside a non-diffusion reenactment decoder.** Adapt SPDMark's LoRA-basis design to LivePortrait's SPADE generator, with the appearance extractor, motion extractor and warping module frozen. No such experiment exists. The compute estimate comes only by analogy to SPDMark's ~8 GPU-hours on latent-diffusion decoders, not from any measurement on LivePortrait, against LivePortrait's original ~12-day 8×A100 training ([SPDMark](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.12090v2); [LivePortrait](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.03168)).

**(4) Build declared-claim binding with biometric cross-checking, the plan's real contribution.** Reviewers will ask why passive fingerprinting plus C2PA is not enough. The answer the student must demonstrate empirically is complementarity. Biometrics lose up to 20 AUC points across generators ([AVAPrintDB](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.26934v2)), while a signed claim from a controlled deployment does not depend on the generator. Conversely, the biometric can contest a forged or replayed claim. The hypothesis is that fusion improves TAR at low FAR under generator shift and attack. It has to be shown, not assumed.

**(5) Attack the biometric adversarially.** No paper attacks avatar fingerprinting or latent puppeteer detectors with replay or retargeting of a victim's recorded motion, with mimicry, or with adversarial latents. AVAPrintDB and Chapariniya et al. explicitly list this as untested ([AVAPrintDB](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.26934v2); [Chapariniya et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.23247v1)). Replay matters most here. If a victim's public video drives the avatar, the motion biometric *passes*, because it really is the victim's motion. Motion biometrics therefore do not prove liveness, and the system needs a session-bound challenge or a capture-time device signature.

**(6) Report EER and TAR@FAR, with self and cross results separate.** This costs nothing, and none of the fingerprinting papers does it; all report AUC only ([AVAPrintDB](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.26934v2); [Unmasking Puppeteers](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.03548v4)).

## A feasible B.Tech build: LivePortrait, AVAPrintDB, pointer payload, offline scope

The manifest is where the plan's claims belong. It should hold a `c2pa.created` action with the IPTC `trainedAlgorithmicMedia` source type. Alongside it go a custom reenactment assertion carrying the target commitment, the declared-driver commitment, the generator and weights hash, the session ID and nonce, and a consent-record reference; a hard binding; and a `c2pa.soft-binding` assertion with one block per watermarked segment ([C2PA 2.4](https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.4/specs/C2PA_Specification.html)). Identity can be represented as a random pseudonymous ID issued at consented enrollment and stored in a private registry. The manifest then carries only a salted or keyed commitment, giving a different value per video so videos stay unlinkable. Disclosure should be tiered: the public sees "AI-generated, consented reenactment", while victims, platforms and regulators can open the commitments. CAWG identity-aggregator patterns support this kind of local identifier ([CAWG identity](https://opensource.contentauthenticity.org/docs/manifest/cawg-id)). For a student prototype, a non-registered soft-binding `alg` is acceptable if documented as a deviation, since VideoSeal is not on the approved list.

The verifier's decision logic replaces the plan's four-row table with three independent checks. The first asks **whether the watermark-resolved manifest is validly signed and bound to *this* content**, which means checking the content descriptor, not just the ID. The second asks **whether the motion biometric accepts the declared driver** at a calibrated threshold against that driver's enrollment. The third asks **whether segment indices are complete and in order**. Each check reports its own uncertainty. Disagreement is flagged, not adjudicated, because the biometric's accuracy (AUC 0.85–0.97, with no published EER or TAR@FAR) is far too uncertain to convict anyone.

## Evaluate as a security system, not a quality demo

The evaluation must state attacker knowledge explicitly, following VideoMarkBench: white-box (public extractor and weights), black-box (scores or labels from a verification API) and no-box (common transforms) ([VideoMarkBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.21620)). **Benign robustness** should sweep H.264 and H.265 at CRF 23, 30 and 40, conferencing transcodes, crop, resize, brightness, and Gaussian noise up to σ = 0.15. That noise level alone drives VideoSeal's FNR to about 1. **Removal** should include per-frame VAE and diffusion regeneration, which strips over 99% of several pixel watermarks on images ([Zhao et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.01953v3)) and drops Stable Signature's TPR to about 0.001 ([WAVES](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.08573v3)), as well as white-box PGD through the public extractor. Regeneration has never been run against a video watermark, so even negative results are new. **Forgery** should measure the false-positive rate for transplanting a valid mark onto clean or other-session video, PGD forgery, and averaging the residual across many outputs. Copy and collusion attacks are untested in the 2023–2026 video literature. **Temporal** tests should cover random and block deletion, insertion of foreign or other-session frames, reordering, splicing two sessions, and 30→24→30 and 30→60 fps conversion with interpolation, reporting decode robustness and tamper-detection TPR and FPR separately. **Biometric** attacks should cover zero-effort impostors, replay or retargeting of the target's own public motion, and expression suppression. A practical success criterion requires that the attack removes the mark, stays within a quality budget, *and* evades forensic laundering detectors, which flag at least 99% of image-domain removal outputs at 1% FPR ([Goonatilake & Ateniese](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09203)). For talking heads, the quality budget should be semantic: an attack that removes the mark but destroys CSIM or expression fidelity has not succeeded.

Several metrics need care. For the watermark, report bit accuracy and TPR at FPR of 10⁻², 10⁻³ and 10⁻⁴, and state the frame-to-video aggregation rule; logit-median beats mean in VideoMarkBench. A binomial null for 96 bits needs at least 72 correct bits to reach FPR below 10⁻⁶ (tail ≈ 4.8×10⁻⁷), but learned extractors produce correlated bits. Thresholds must therefore be calibrated empirically on real unwatermarked talking-head video. By the rule of three, claiming FPR ≤ 10⁻⁴ needs about 30,000 clean negatives. Report Clopper–Pearson intervals throughout. Imperceptibility needs PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, VMAF and temporal LPIPS. VideoSeal's authors warn that artifacts can be visible even at high scores ([VideoSeal](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.09492)). The fidelity cost of watermarking should be measured as a delta against LivePortrait's own protocol and baselines: self-reenactment on TalkingHead-1KH (PSNR 32.01, LPIPS 0.066, CSIM 0.913), and cross-reenactment with the first 50 FFHQ images as sources, scored by CSIM, AED and APD via SMIRK, plus gaze MAE and FID ([LivePortrait](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.03168)).

For the biometric, report AUC with bootstrap CIs, as AVAPrintDB does, plus EER and TAR at FAR of 10⁻² and 10⁻³, broken down per generator, cross-generator, cross-dataset, per demographic group, and self versus cross. Data comes from AVAPrintDB, which is public on GitHub and Zenodo ([AVAPrintDB repo](https://github.com/BiometricsAI/AVAPrintDB); [Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/20548979)). The BiDA GAGAvatar benchmark is a lighter alternative ([GAGAvatar-Benchmark](https://github.com/BiDAlab/GAGAvatar-Benchmark)). NVFAIR is available only by request, carries per-file consent-to-display flags, and its LIA and TPS renders are released for the test set only ([NVFAIR](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/nxp/nvfair/)). The headline experiment is the plan's own Stage 4 ablation, scored on TAR at low FAR under generator shift and under each attack class.

## Regulation now rewards exactly this layered design

**India** is the most immediate jurisdiction. The IT Rules amendments notified on 10 February 2026 and in force from 20 February 2026 define "synthetically generated information" broadly enough to cover reenactment. Intermediaries offering SGI tools must label it clearly and prominently and, "where feasible", embed permanent metadata or unique identifiers that identify the generating resource, and they must prevent removal of labels or metadata. Significant social media intermediaries must verify users' SGI declarations with technical measures rather than relying on the declarations alone. Takedown windows shrink to 2 hours for impersonation and morphed images and 3 hours after official orders ([Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/technology-quotient/india-targets-deepfakes-and-ai-generated-content-key-changes-under-meitys-2026-102mjwn); [MediaNama](https://www.medianama.com/2026/02/223-meity-amendments-it-rules-synthetic-media-deepfakes/)).

Two Indian measures are less settled. A **draft** amendment would require a continuous visible label for the full duration of SGI video; comments closed on 7 May 2026 and no notification has been found ([MeitY notice](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2026/04/ec197f1206279efb4964965f0dede6c1.pdf)). An 8 October 2026 MeitY advisory asking platforms to label manipulated or misleading content is reported by a single source ([MediaNama](https://www.medianama.com/2026/10/223-meity-labelling-social-media-synthetic-content/)). Separately, the DPDP Rules 2025 phase in verifiable-consent and notice duties around May 2027 ([PIB](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2025/nov/doc20251117695301.pdf)). Collecting both the target's face and the driver's motion is personal-data processing, so the conservative design takes itemised consent from both people. A research prototype is not itself an intermediary, but its watermark ID is a practical way to make metadata "permanent", because the ID survives metadata stripping.

**In the EU**, AI Act Article 50 transparency duties apply from 2 August 2026. According to law-firm summaries, the voluntary Code of Practice published around 10 June 2026 asks generative-AI providers for at least two machine-readable marking techniques, such as signed metadata plus an imperceptible watermark, and a detection mechanism free in principle to regulators and researchers. Deployers must visibly label deepfakes, and provider marking does not discharge that duty. Watermark interoperability is due by 2 February 2027. Fines reach €15M or 3% of worldwide turnover. A deferral to 2 December 2026 for systems already on the market is reported but its adoption is unconfirmed. The Paul Weiss summary does not name C2PA, and it notes that the technology "is not yet up to the task" for parts of the obligations ([Paul Weiss](https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-memos/eu-finalises-transparency-rules-for-ai-generated-content); [Addleshaw Goddard](https://www.addleshawgoddard.com/en/insights/insights-briefings/2026/technology/eu-ai-act-code-practice-marking-labelling-ai-generated-content/)).

**In the US** there is no general marking mandate. The FTC began enforcing the TAKE IT DOWN Act's 48-hour removal duty for non-consensual intimate imagery, including AI deepfakes, on 19 May 2026 ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/05/ftc-begins-enforcing-take-it-down-act)). The NO FAKES Act, which would create a licensable federal digital-replica right, is reported reintroduced in May 2026 but not enacted ([SAG-AFTRA](https://www.sagaftra.org/contracts-industry-resources/member-resources/artificial-intelligence)). California AB 2602 and New York's digital-replica law govern performer consent contracts ([MoFo](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/250922-digital-avatars-deep-dive-series-navigating)). For a reenactment service, these consent-and-licensing rules map directly onto the plan's "authorized driver" concept.

**China** has applied its Labeling Measures together with mandatory standard GB 45438-2025 since 1 September 2025. They require explicit visible labels plus implicit labels embedded in metadata that carry content attributes, the provider name and a content number. Platforms must check for implicit labels and add explicit ones where missing. Reported issuance dates vary across sources ([Loeb & Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2025/03/chinas-ai-labeling-measures-and-mandatory-national-standards-take-effect-september-1)).

One layered design, a visible "AI" label plus a signed C2PA manifest plus an invisible watermark resolving to the manifest, maps onto all four regimes. It meets the EU's two machine-readable techniques, India's label plus non-removable identifier, and China's explicit plus implicit labels, with the manifest ID serving as the content number. Identity-revealing fields stay behind access control.

## Conclusion

The literature changes what this project should claim. The defensible contribution is not a watermark that "knows" who drove a video; no watermark can know that, and inferring it from motion only relabels the biometric. The contribution is a verification protocol in which a controlled generator *attests* to a declared driver and target under signed consent, and an independent motion biometric *contests* that attestation. The open question is whether the two together beat either alone under generator shift and replay. That framing also limits what the project can promise. It offers positive attestation for cooperating services, not detection of rogue reenactments made with public LivePortrait weights. Saying so up front is what will make the work credible to reviewers and regulators.

The cheapest results are also the most novel. No one has measured whether existing face watermarks survive LivePortrait, GAGAvatar or HunyuanPortrait, or whether watermarking erodes the micro-motion cues that avatar fingerprinting depends on. Both experiments run on released code and the public AVAPrintDB, and either could anchor a workshop paper before any custom decoder training begins. The decoder-LoRA watermark and the full fusion ablation are the ambitious second half. They are worth attempting only after the threat model, the payload-as-pointer design, and EER-based evaluation are fixed in writing.
